BUS331 Investment Project · Phase 3

Build Your Client Portfolios

Use your economic review, client IPS statements, and bond and equity research to select investments, measure risk, and recommend portfolio weights.

Your task: Select 10 different securities for each of your three clients. Build their price histories with Excel’s STOCKHISTORY function, calculate risk, compare your initial allocation with Solver, and recommend final weights.

Submit one team Excel workbook. The final oral presentation bringing all project phases together is Phase 4.

Your work sequence — when to run Solver

  1. Build the team CME with one change for Equity, Bond and Cash (Step 1).
  2. Select 10 securities per client, classify them, and enter the client’s IPS inputs (Step 2).
  3. Build the historical risk analysis: collect 61 closes, calculate 60 returns, and complete the covariance matrix, correlation and statistics (Steps 3–4).
  4. Propose initial weights and set limits, then copy the initial weights into the Solver column (Step 5).
  5. Run Solver separately for each client to find a lower-variance allocation under the client’s return goal and constraints (Step 6).
  6. Evaluate Solver, recommend final weights, and stress test before submitting the workbook (Steps 7–8).

Run Solver at Step 6, after Steps 1–5 are complete for that client. Your own initial allocation comes first; Solver provides a comparison before your final recommendation.

Before you begin

  • Download the revised Phase 3 Portfolio Student Template (.xlsx). Use desktop Excel with STOCKHISTORY access and the Solver add-in.
  • Have your economic review, three IPS statements, and completed security research available. Use your existing work to justify decisions.
  • On Summary, enter the team name and analysis date in the yellow cells. The workbook sets the common history window using the last completed month. Keep that analysis date fixed for the submission.

Cell guide: Yellow = inputs or explanations. Purple = formulas you write. Other cells contain provided calculations or links. Enter percentages as 5% or 0.05. Use an explicit zero when a required numeric estimate is zero; do not replace missing observations with zeros.

The workbook has 13 tabs: Summary, CME, Client 1–3, Stress Test, History 1–3, Risk 1–3, and Reference. History 1 and Risk 1 support Client 1; the same numbering applies to Clients 2 and 3.

Step 1 — Build the team CME

Enter one general change for Equity, Bond and Cash. Every selected sector or bond category keeps its own historical baseline and receives the matching class change. No separate sector forecasts are required.

  1. Review the separate Reference tab. Its provided table lists each equity sector, broad equity, bond category and cash proxy with its historical annual mean, annual sample SD, source and history window. It is a data reference only; leave its values unchanged. The statistics use the same five published calendar-year NAV total returns, 2021–2025. The proxy ETFs and any scope limitations are identified in each row.
  2. Enter class changes in CME F9:F11. Use signed percentage points: -2% means two points below each matching category’s historical mean; +1% means one point above it. Enter 0% to make no change. Leave no class change blank. Use your growth, inflation and interest-rate outlook to justify the three changes.
  3. Calculate the broad reference examples in G9:G11. D/E link to broad Equity, Bond and Cash proxy statistics from Reference. In G9 enter =IF(AND(ISNUMBER(D9),ISNUMBER(F9)),D9+F9,"") and fill down. This demonstrates the adjustment rule; it does not assign the same final return to every sector.
  4. Calculate broad reference downside in H9:H11. In H9 enter =IF(AND(ISNUMBER(G9),ISNUMBER(E9)),G9-$C$6*E9,"") and fill down. Keep the common factor of 2 in C6. On the Client sheets, the workbook uses each selected category’s own adjusted return and SD for its downside.
  5. Explain the changes in I9:I11. State why each class should be above, below or equal to its usual reference return. Cite the economic evidence and source/date. An unchanged outlook still requires an explanation. Resolve the status messages in J9:J11.

Example using illustrative numbers: If Information Technology’s historical mean is 10% and the Equity change is -2%, its adjusted expectation is 8%. If Health Care’s mean is 7%, that same Equity change produces 5%. A +1% Bond change adds one percentage point to each bond category’s own historical mean. These changes are additive, not a multiplication by 98% or 101%.

Keep each category’s SD unchanged. A shift in the expected return does not change the supplied historical SD. Category downside = category historical mean + class change − 2 × category SD. For example, the 8% adjusted return above with a 15% category SD gives a downside of -22%.

Use the correct basis. The reference mean and SD are calculated from five annual observations, a short classroom sample; do not multiply them by 12 or SQRT(12). These proxy statistics are not a long-run forecast and may differ from the selected security. Your separate covariance exercise uses 60 monthly security price returns and its own annualization.

Interpret downside as a stress scenario. The two-SD rule is not a guaranteed loss limit, historical maximum drawdown or specified probability. Applying it to all categories together does not establish a joint probability. A negative cash result may reflect a limitation of this symmetric rule and changing income, rather than a negative Treasury-bill payoff.

Income is already included. Category baselines and adjusted expectations are total returns. Do not add a separate dividend, coupon or distribution yield.

CME research resources

  • Reference tab — public iShares/BlackRock fact sheets: Each row links to its proxy’s source and reports the document date. The underlying annual NAV returns are also provided on that tab. You do not need to collect another dataset or forecast each sector separately.
  • FactSet Earnings Insight — October 30, 2026: Optional context on earnings, valuations and analyst expectations. Price-target upside excludes income and is not directly comparable to the total-return baseline. You are not required to forecast its individual sectors.
  • Schwab Monthly Stock Sector Outlook: Optional context on economic drivers and sector risks. Ratings describe relative six- to 12-month performance, rather than numerical return forecasts. Use relevant commentary to support your three class-level changes.

Step 2 — Select 10 securities for each client

Complete the IPS inputs and selection table on each Client sheet.

  1. Transfer the IPS. Enter the client name, portfolio value, annual total-return goal, cash minimum, equity and bond maximums, horizon/liquidity needs, IPS reference, and scenario loss tripwire. Enter the loss tripwire as a positive percentage. Add the optional annual price-risk SD limit only when justified for that measure.
  2. Complete rows 17–26. For all 10 securities, enter the name, exchange/ticker identifier, asset class (Equity, Bond or Cash), descriptive sector/category, client role and CME fit, research reference, and as-of date. Columns G/H automatically show the selected category’s Reference mean and SD; no income estimates are required.
  3. Classify the actual exposure. Select the relevant equity sector for a sector-focused equity investment. Use Broad equity for a diversified equity fund and an appropriate fixed-income or cash category for those exposures. Explain why each security fits this client and the shared asset-class outlook. The sector/category determines the historical baseline; its asset class determines which common adjustment applies. You do not enter another sector forecast.
  4. Check distinctness and coverage. Each client needs 10 different instruments. A second listing of the same company or fund is not a different security. Use consistent exchange:ticker identifiers. Securities must have sufficient STOCKHISTORY coverage; suitable traded funds can implement bond or cash exposures.

The same security may appear for more than one client when justified. You need 10 distinct securities within each client portfolio, rather than 30 unique securities across the team. Resolve the selection-status messages in column L.

Step 3 — Collect prices with STOCKHISTORY

Use the reusable download station on History 1, History 2, and History 3. Repeat the following for every security selected for that client.

  1. Choose the selected ticker in C6. The first price month in C7 and request end date in G6 link to Summary.
  2. Enter this formula in the purple cell B16. Use ordinary straight quotation marks, then set the download switch G8 to Retrieve.
=IF($G$8="Retrieve",STOCKHISTORY($C$6,$C$7,$G$6,2,0,0,1),"")

The final arguments request monthly data, no returned headers, and Date plus Close. Leave the spill area B16:C76 clear except for the formula in B16.

  1. Validate the result. G16 must show 61 closes and G18 must show 61 aligned dates. STOCKHISTORY labels each monthly observation with the first day of the month; its Close is the final trading close in that month. Exclude the current incomplete month.
  2. Save the prices. Copy only C16:C76. In the snapshot table, locate the matching ticker in row 82, select the first price cell in row 83, and use Paste Special > Values. Each security gets 61 closes in its column within C83:L143. Keep the supplied dates in column B.
  3. Record that column’s source details. Enter captured ticker in row 145, first returned month in row 146, last returned month in row 147, retrieval date in row 148, and source/saved-record reference in row 150. Use first-of-month dates for the returned-month metadata. Confirm Ready in row 151.
  4. Set G8 back to Hold before changing the ticker. Repeat for all 10 securities, preserving the earlier snapshots and the formula in B16. Complete the corresponding history sheet for each client.

Why 61 prices? You need one baseline close plus 60 subsequent closes to calculate 60 monthly price returns. If the analysis date or a ticker changes, rebuild the affected snapshots and calculations.

If the history does not spill: Check that G8 says Retrieve, the ticker and dates are valid, and the spill area is empty. If Excel inserts an @ immediately after the equals sign, re-enter the formula in Excel without that @ so it returns the full array. Resolve #SPILL!, #BUSY!, or source-access errors before copying. If coverage is insufficient, revise the selection or discuss an approved alternative with the instructor.

Step 4 — Calculate returns and covariance

Complete the purple cells on Risk 1, Risk 2, and Risk 3 using each client’s own selected securities and saved prices.

  • Monthly price returns, C10:L69: current close divided by preceding close, minus 1. For Client 1’s first return, the underlying calculation is ='History 1'!C84/'History 1'!C83-1. Check for two numeric, positive prices before dividing. Fill down 60 months and across 10 securities.
  • Annual covariance, C76:L85: use COVARIANCE.S for each pair of monthly return series, multiplied by 12. Build a 10 × 10 matrix. Check symmetry; the diagonal is annual variance.
  • Correlation, C91:L100: use CORREL on the same aligned series. The diagonal should equal 1.
  • Statistics, C105:F114: use AVERAGE and STDEV.S. Annual arithmetic mean = monthly mean × 12. Annual SD = monthly sample SD × SQRT(12).

Keep the ticker order consistent across returns, matrices, and weights. Confirm 60 return observations for every security. Explain one useful diversification pairing in the response area.

Interpret the basis correctly: These historical calculations measure price-return risk, without reconstructing distributions or reinvestment. CME total returns already include income, while the security covariance estimate does not reconstruct distributions. Discuss this limitation for income-oriented holdings. Annualization assumes sufficiently stable monthly returns without material serial correlation.

Step 5 — Set initial weights and limits

Complete this step after the client’s historical risk analysis is ready. On each Client sheet:

  1. Enter your judgment-based initial weights in C32:C41. All 10 positions must receive positive weights, totaling 100%. Explain the initial decision in the response below row 95.
  2. Enter justified positive minimum weights in F32:F41 and appropriate maximums in G32:G41. There is no preset assignment-wide minimum percentage. The limits must allow a feasible portfolio and meaningful positions.
  3. Review the linked expected total returns in column I and downside total returns in column J. Confirm that each security uses its own sector/category reference mean plus the matching Equity, Bond or Cash change. Its downside must use that category’s SD. Holdings in the same class can therefore have different expected and downside returns.
  4. Copy C32:C41 and paste values into D32:D41. Preserve column C as the initial allocation. Solver will change only column D.

Step 6 — Run Solver for each client

Purpose: Find the lowest modeled portfolio variance while meeting the return goal, security weight limits, and client constraints. A mathematical result still needs your investment judgment.

Before you click Solve

Check these prerequisites for the client you are solving:

  • The shared CME J9:J11 statuses say Complete, and all 10 selections on the Client sheet show Complete in L17:L26.
  • All 10 source histories show Ready in History C151:L151. The matching Risk sheet has 60 aligned returns per security and a complete, numeric 10 × 10 annual covariance matrix in C76:L85. Correlation and statistics from Step 4 are also complete.
  • The client’s IPS goals and limits are entered. Initial weights in C32:C41 are positive and total 100%; minimum and maximum weights in F32:G41 are entered and justified.
  • Initial weights have been copied as values into D32:D41. The linked expected-return and variance calculations are numeric, and missing-data or formula errors are resolved.

The initial allocation may fall short of the return goal or breach an allocation limit; Solver will attempt to resolve those constraints. Keep the CME and historical data fixed during the run. Solver changes the weights in column D.

A. Enable Solver

  • Windows: File > Options > Add-ins. At Manage, select Excel Add-ins, choose Go, check Solver Add-in, and select OK.
  • Mac: Tools > Excel Add-ins. Check Solver Add-In and select OK.

Return to the client worksheet and open Data > Solver. Use desktop Excel for this exercise. See Microsoft’s Solver setup instructions if the button is missing.

B. Enter the model

  1. Set Set Objective to $D$46 and select Min. D46 is annual price-return variance. Select Min rather than a specified target value.
  2. Set By Changing Variable Cells to $D$32:$D$41.
  3. Under the constraints list, select Add. Enter the cell reference, relationship, and constraint shown in each row below. Add each constraint, then return to the main Solver window.

Enter 1 for the full-investment constraint: Excel stores 100% as 1. For the other limits, select the indicated cells or ranges rather than retyping their displayed percentages. Check that all references belong to the client you are solving.

C. Solve and check

  1. Choose GRG Nonlinear as the solving method, then select Solve.
  2. Read the Solver Results message. If it reports a feasible solution, choose Keep Solver Solution and select OK.
  3. Inspect D44:D50 and the checks in D69:D76. Confirm 100% total weight, 10 positive positions, return-goal compliance, and all applicable limits. Compare expected return and risk with column C.
  4. Record Solver’s message, key weight changes, and your accept/adjust/reject decision in the response below row 98.
  5. Repeat on Client 2 and Client 3, using each client’s own inputs, histories, covariance matrix, and starting weights. Verify the model references each time.

If Solver fails: Check for missing histories, unfinished covariance formulas, formula errors, unfinished asset-class CME assumptions, conflicting minimums/maximums, or an unattainable return goal. A stopped or infeasible run is not a completed optimization. Record the message and explain the conflict. Do not quietly change the IPS to force a solution.

Step 7 — Recommend final weights and stress test

Begin this step after reviewing and recording the client’s Solver result. Compare all three allocations: C = your initial proposal, D = Solver result, E = final recommendation.

  1. Enter your final recommendation in E32:E41. If you accept Solver, copy D32:D41 and paste values into E32:E41. If you adjust it, explain why. Preserve both the initial and Solver allocations.
  2. Keep all 10 holdings at positive weights, totaling 100%, and review final dollar allocations. Check final-column IPS results, concentration, sector overlap, costs, liquidity, and suitability.
  3. Explain the final decision, one rejected alternative, the main trade-off, and any unresolved reservation in the response below row 101.
  4. On Stress Test, review each client’s downside total return, dollar loss, and loss-tripwire comparison. Explain the client-goal consequence, monitoring trigger, and correction or reservation. Revise final weights if needed and re-test.

A one-period downside estimate is different from historical maximum drawdown and does not guarantee a maximum future loss. A second downside optimization is not required.

When to rerun Solver: If you change the CME, selected securities, historical inputs, or IPS/weight constraints after the run, update the affected calculations and run Solver again before finalizing the comparison. A security or history change requires rebuilding the affected returns and covariance matrix first. If you only adjust final weights in column E, recheck the IPS and downside results and explain your judgment.
